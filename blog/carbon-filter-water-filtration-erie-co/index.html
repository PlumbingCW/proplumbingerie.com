Carbon Filter Water Filtration for Erie, CO Homes

A carbon filter is the most common home water filter. It uses activated carbon, a porous material that grabs certain chemicals as water flows through. People install one to reduce chlorine taste and odor, and some models reduce other substances too.

This guide explains what carbon does well, where it falls short and how to pick a system for your house.

What A Carbon Filter Does

Activated carbon works by adsorption. Molecules stick to the surface of the carbon as water passes through. It is effective on chlorine, many tastes and odors and some organic chemicals. It does not remove dissolved minerals, so it does not soften water.

Not all carbon filters are alike. Block carbon filters packed tightly can capture more than loose granular carbon. Look for a filter that lists what it is tested to reduce, and check that the claims are certified by a recognized testing body.

Pitcher, Under-Sink Or Whole-House

Pitcher or faucet mount

Low cost, no plumbing, small volume. Good for drinking water only.

Under-sink

Connects to the cold line and gives filtered water at a dedicated tap. Needs space in the cabinet.

Whole-house

Filters water at the point of entry. Reduces chlorine in showers and laundry. Needs a larger unit and a bypass.

The best choice depends on what bothers you. If it is the taste of drinking water, an under-sink filter is usually enough. If it is the smell of shower water or dry skin after bathing, whole-house carbon may help.

Maintenance Matters

A filter that is overdue for replacement stops working and can release what it captured. Follow the maker's schedule, which depends on the volume of water and the quality of the supply. Write the change date on the housing.

Whole-house systems should have a bypass valve and a pressure gauge so you can see when flow drops. A clogged filter lowers pressure everywhere in the house.

Test Before You Buy

A home test kit or a lab test tells you what is in your water. Without a test, you are buying a solution without knowing the problem. If you see scale on fixtures, a water softener may be the better first step.

Choosing By Certification

A filter label should say what the filter is certified to reduce and by which testing body. Claims such as improves taste are vague. Claims that cite a standard number and list contaminants are more reliable. If the label lists nothing specific, treat it as a basic taste filter.

Match the filter to your goal. A filter certified for chlorine reduction does not necessarily address other substances. Test your water first, then pick a filter that lists the substances you care about.

Installing Under The Sink

An under-sink filter tees into the cold supply and sends filtered water to a small separate faucet. The housing needs room to unscrew the cartridge for changes. Leave a few inches of clearance below it.

Install a shutoff before the filter so you can change cartridges without shutting off the house. Check every fitting for drips after the first day.

Whole-House Placement

A whole-house filter goes after the main shutoff and the pressure regulator, and before the water heater. Leave space around it for cartridge changes and install a bypass. Label the date of installation on the housing.

If the home has a softener, the carbon filter usually goes ahead of it to protect the softener resin from chlorine.
