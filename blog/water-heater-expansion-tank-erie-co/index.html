Do You Need a Water Heater Expansion Tank in Erie, CO?

Water expands when it is heated. In an open plumbing system, the extra volume pushes back into the street supply and no one notices. In a closed system, with a check valve or pressure reducing valve on the incoming line, the extra volume has nowhere to go.

A thermal expansion tank gives that water a place to go, and it keeps pressure from climbing past what pipes and fixtures can take.

What The Tank Does

An expansion tank is a small steel tank with a rubber bladder inside. One side holds air under pressure, and the other connects to your cold water line near the heater. When heating raises the pressure, the bladder flexes and absorbs the extra volume.

Without it, pressure can spike each time the heater runs. The relief valve may drip or discharge, fixtures may wear out sooner and the heater itself can be stressed.

Signs a closed system needs an expansion tank

  • The temperature and pressure relief valve drips or discharges periodically
  • Pressure on a gauge climbs when the heater fires and falls when the tap opens
  • Banging pipes right after a heating cycle
  • Toilets that run or fill valves that fail more often than expected
  • A backflow preventer or pressure reducing valve on the main line

Sizing And Setting The Tank

1

Measure pressure

Read the static water pressure with a gauge.

2

Pick the size

Tank size depends on the heater volume and the pressure.

3

Match the precharge

Set the air side to equal the house water pressure before connecting.

4

Install and support

Mount the tank on the cold line near the heater with a support strap if the unit is heavy.

The air charge is the detail most often missed. A tank shipped at a default pressure that does not match the house will not work as intended. A plumber checks and adjusts it.

Replacement And Maintenance

A waterlogged tank has lost its bladder or air charge. Tap it, and if it sounds full all the way up, it has failed. Press the air valve too, since water coming out means the bladder broke. Expansion tanks last years but not forever, so check them when you replace a heater.

Thermal Expansion And Pressure Limits

Plumbing codes commonly limit static water pressure to 80 psi, and many homes run lower. Thermal expansion can add many pounds of pressure in a closed system within minutes of the heater firing. That is why the relief valve may open even when static pressure looks normal.

The pressure reducing valve is another part of the story. If it fails, supply pressure rises. A gauge on a hose bib helps you see whether the problem is supply pressure or expansion.

Common Mistakes

What goes wrong with expansion tanks

  • Installing a tank without matching its air charge to house pressure
  • Mounting it so its weight hangs from a copper pipe
  • Putting it on the hot side instead of the cold side
  • Skipping the shutoff and drain that make future replacement simple
  • Never checking it after the first year

A tank that fails does not announce itself. Check it each year with the system off and the pressure relieved. An expansion tank that sounds solid on both ends is waterlogged and needs replacing.
